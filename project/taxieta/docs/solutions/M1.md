# Milestone 1 — solution walkthrough

**Run it:** `taxieta solve m1` → `reports/solutions/m1.md`
**Reference design doc:** `docs/design_doc.md` (compare it section by section with yours)

## What the solution does
1. Fits the heuristic (median per zone pair × hour-of-week with back-off) on Jan–Apr 2019 and
   scores May 2019, next to the zero-rule baseline. That's your **bar to beat** for every later milestone.
2. Estimates peak load from the busiest minute, scaled by the ingest sample fraction, to size
   the service and the M6 load test.
3. Summarizes the label: percentiles, skew, LongTrip rate. These justify a log target and robust loss.

## What to expect on real data (qualitative)
- The heuristic already beats the zero rule by a wide margin: location and time explain most
  of the variance. ML has to win on the *residual* (live traffic, interactions, sparse zone pairs).
- Durations are strongly right-skewed. A few trips are hours long (jams, meter left running).
- Peak minute ≫ average. Sizing for the average is a classic mistake.

## Learning-log answers
1. **Why is “% within ±20%” closer to the business than RMSE?** Riders judge an ETA
   *relative* to the trip: 5 minutes off a 10-minute trip feels broken, 5 minutes off a
   60-minute trip is fine. RMSE is absolute and dominated by the few longest trips, so it
   optimizes for rare outliers instead of the typical rider's experience. A bounded, relative,
   per-trip “was it right?” rate maps directly onto “did the rider trust it?”.
2. **ML metric up, business down:** e.g., MAE improves because the model gets better on short
   Manhattan trips, while it systematically *underestimates* long airport trips. Riders heading
   to flights miss them, dispatch chains break, cancellations rise. Or the model is more
   accurate but its ETA jumps on every refresh, and riders lose trust (Ch. 11 consistency).
3. **Percentiles, not the average:** latency is a distribution. A handful of slow requests
   (GC pauses, cold caches, long feature fetches) are invisible in the mean but hit real users.
   Often those are the most valuable ones (Ch. 1: heavy users have the slowest requests). p99 tells
   you what 1 in 100 riders experiences.
4. **Regression with destination as input vs. one classifier per destination:** one model with
   (pickup, dropoff, time) as features shares statistical strength across zone pairs, handles
   pairs it rarely saw, and needs no retraining when a zone is added. Per-destination models
   fragment the data (thousands of tiny models, most with too few samples) and must be rebuilt
   whenever the output space changes. That's the same argument as Ch. 2's “predict the next app”:
   put the item into the input instead of the output.
